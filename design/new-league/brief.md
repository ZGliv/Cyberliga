# Cyberliga / A new league

Prepared September 6, 2026. All design choices below are proposals for review.

## 1. The product we are designing

An autonomous fictional basketball league whose athletes become recognizable through decisions, movement and shared history. Open on the match. Help the viewer see an intention, anticipate a contest, understand the result, and remember who caused it.

The immediate creative milestone is six athletes and one portrait court. The initial competition prototype has two clubs, three players per club and one arena. A four-club, twelve-athlete league follows only after viewers voluntarily return for another match.

The consumer experience begins with spectating. The future operator proposition is an authoritative competition and data feed with separately scoped distribution approval, not a promise that a prototype can accept bets.

## 2. Art directions

A - Graphic sculpture (recommended hypothesis): matte simplified 3D masses, planar facial structure, readable limbs, restrained texture and a clear jersey silhouette. Strong physical contact and controlled exaggeration. Test for generic mascot appearance and hand/ball legibility.

B - Handmade miniatures: painted foam/wood forms and cloth uniforms. Potential warmth and tactile humor; generated study may drift toward excessive realism and surface noise. Treat it as an exploration, not an approved cast.

C - Sports comic: crisp contours, limited cel shading, graphic shape design and illustrated attitude. Potentially strongest small-scale contrast; assess consistency across camera angles and contact animation.

Judge each at full figure and at phone size. Existing neon, capsule bodies and photoreal humans are not design targets. Do not choose a direction on a portrait illustration alone.

## 3. Provisional clubs and cast

**Pittsburgh Kiln**: rust-orange, midnight ink, warm cream. Patient and physical. Its mark uses a kiln-door form, with no borrowed professional-sports imagery.

**Oakland Switch**: deep teal, cream, warm gold. Quick defensive exchanges and opportunistic pressure. Its mark uses two offset bars.

The recommended cast uses simplified nonhuman biped anatomy with bounded variation. Jersey numbers, face shape, posture and rhythm provide identity. Avoid simulated fur, extravagant appendages and six unrelated skeletons. Visible reach and collision behavior must agree.

## 4. Court and broadcast

One compact municipal hall: light maple court, brick and cream walls, timber roof beams, broad overhead light and three shallow spectator rows. Keep spectator contrast lower than athlete contrast. Use a rust key, dark clean markings and an uncluttered run-off boundary.

Portrait opens directly into the match. A short header and score strip identify teams, score, game clock and shot clock. The court owns most of the screen. One concise contextual line may identify the current player or completed action. Schedule and history sit below the viewing area.

Landscape uses a separate stable camera with additional lateral context. Both orientations consume the same official match. Rotation cannot reset play. No required rotation, spectator camera controls, pause, restart or gameplay inputs.

Camera tests: preserve all relevant players and ball; avoid occlusion during screens and rebounds; do not cut during shot flight; keep possession direction stable. Use a closer view only when the action permits it. Replays are marked and sourced from the actual match. In the prototype, show them after the match or during an existing stoppage long enough to contain the entire replay. Never hide a live possession or lengthen a two-second restart for a replay.

The generated court scenes are composition studies. They do not prove geometry accuracy, six-player tracking, frame rate, motion continuity or one-to-one event fidelity.

## 5. One possession worth remembering

Illustrative 12-second decision storyboard followed by flight and reaction, not actual simulation footage. Pittsburgh trails 12-14 with 0:42 on the game clock and 12 on the shot clock.

0-3 seconds: Nix reaches for an expected pass. Latch keeps his dribble and turns toward Brunt's planted screen. The viewer sees the gamble create an opening.

3-6 seconds: Toll helps toward Latch. Brunt rolls into the vacated space. Latch chooses the pocket pass instead of a contested finish.

6-9 seconds: Sable leaves Zip to meet Brunt near the basket. Brunt begins his hook, recognizes the second defender and passes out.

9-12 seconds: Sable recovers. Zip adjusts his feet and releases the three at 11.8 seconds (game clock 0:30.2, shot clock 0.2). This final decision beat ends with the ball in flight.

12-15 seconds: the shot drops at approximately 13.0 seconds, making it 15-14 with 0:29 remaining. The game clock stops on the made basket. A short Zip identification and reaction use the two-second automatic restart window; Oakland restarts at approximately 15.0 seconds with a fresh shot clock. No replay interrupts the next possession.

The scene is a demonstration of desired causality, not a guaranteed move sequence. Alternate outcomes must include a defender staying home, a deflected pass, a late release, a blocked shot and a miss/rebound. The engine must not force this ending or a close score.

Motion acceptance: dribble remains connected to hand intent; screen feet plant before contact; catch precedes control; gather precedes release; rim/backboard/net response matches the ball; players land and recover with weight. Timing must preserve legal sport state.

## 6. Prototype competition rules

Experimental rules, not a claim of FIBA compliance.

- Two teams of three, one basket, no substitutions or player elimination.
- Regulation is 4:00 of live playing time. Clock stops at baskets, whistles and free throws. Automatic restarts take two seconds. Actual broadcast duration exceeds four minutes and must be measured; an approximately six-minute show is a pacing hypothesis.
- Baskets are two points inside the arc and three outside; touching the arc counts as inside. Free throws are one point. No early target-score finish.
- Shot clock is 12 seconds. Every defensive rebound or steal that establishes new control starts a fresh 12, and clearing consumes that time. Release before expiry permits the shot to finish. A rim touch followed by offensive control resets to 12; otherwise the offensive rebound retains the remaining clock. If the clock has expired and a non-rim miss returns to the offense, it is a turnover rather than a zero-clock live possession.
- Opening possession is selected once by the match authority and recorded. After a basket or dead-ball possession change, restart from the fixed check point beyond the arc.
- Defensive rebounds and steals require clearing: the carrier controls the ball with both feet beyond the arc. A possession gained there is immediately cleared. A shot before clearing is a turnover.
- Out-of-bounds goes to the club that did not touch the ball last. Dead-ball restarts reset the shot clock to 12.
- Offensive foul: turnover and no basket. Nonshooting defensive foul: restart; from the fourth team defensive foul, award two free throws instead. Missed shooting foul: two or three free throws. Made shooting foul: basket plus one. The final missed free throw is reboundable. Team foul counts continue into overtime.
- At regulation expiry, finish a previously released shot and associated foul penalties. If tied, sudden-death overtime begins with possession to the club that did not open regulation. First valid scoring event ends overtime; unused free throws are not taken after the winning point. Record this prototype convention explicitly.
- Technical stalls, ambiguous state and indefinite overtime are operational exceptions: do not fabricate a result. Define suspension/void handling before any operator pilot. A time cap for the entertainment prototype remains a product decision, not a hidden tie-breaker.

## 7. One official history

Versioned rules, roster and agent policies feed one renderer-free authoritative simulation. Ordered events and checkpoints produce every score, replay, statistic and league record. Browser rendering interpolates released state and cannot determine the official result.

Separate sporting randomness from camera, crowd, sound and other presentation randomness. Keep future entropy and unreleased result state private. Train any future learned policies offline and release governed versions. Define within-match adaptation as part of the approved rules.

An outage means unavailable or suspended according to the match contract. It cannot silently replace a match with generated fallback results. Purchases, fan votes, user losses and operator wagering exposure never affect sporting decisions.

## 8. Validation and sequence

1. Creative review: compare A/B/C, recognize the six silhouettes, assess the portrait composition.
2. Motion study: one dribble/catch, one screen/contact, one shot/rebound, one reaction sequence. Review at phone size before expanding the cast.
3. Competition prototype: two clubs and one full match in simple geometry. Test agency, variation, clocks, clearing, fouls and overtime.
4. Integrated prototype: recommended art direction, one court, two camera layouts, official log and exact replay.
5. Small league: four clubs, twelve athletes, persistent schedule and measured history; only after voluntary repeat viewing.
6. Commercial scope: one jurisdiction and partner type, counsel/lab assessment of the actual outcome design, buyer integration criteria and operating-cost model.

Acceptance evidence: unfamiliar viewers can identify possession and all six athletes, explain why a basket happened, recognize one recurring player tendency and choose to watch a rematch. Measure real mobile usability on iPhone and Android, not only desktop emulation. Engine tests cover transition ownership, clearing, rim/arc/boundary edge cases, before/after buzzer release, fouls, overtime, reconnect and checkpoint restoration. Two clients and exact replays must agree.

The first implementation would be a bounded motion and possession prototype. This package does not authorize a production replacement, deployment, live wagering or a claim of certified fairness.
